# Prime Point Health Final Content Brief

Date: May 27, 2026

Role note: This brief synthesizes competitor research and legal-language review for a later Website Implementation Agent. It is risk-aware content guidance, not legal advice or a determination of compliance.

## Approved Messaging

Prime Point Health should position the site around a consult-first, provider-guided care model rather than around guaranteed access to specific peptides or guaranteed outcomes.

Recommended core positioning:

- Personalized health guidance for adults exploring peptide-focused care.
- Provider-led telehealth reviews based on goals, health history, medications, contraindications, and clinical judgment.
- Peptide therapy, when discussed, should be presented as one possible option after evaluation, not as an automatic treatment path.
- Plans may include education, lifestyle guidance, follow-up, and, when clinically appropriate, prescription options.
- Eligibility varies, prescriptions are not guaranteed, and results vary.

Preferred primary message:

"Start with a provider-led review of your goals, health history, and questions about peptide-focused care. If appropriate, a licensed clinician can discuss available options, risks, limitations, and next steps."

Preferred trust line:

"Medical intake • Licensed-provider review • Prescription only when appropriate"

Preferred CTA language:

- "Request consult"
- "Schedule a consultation"
- "Talk with a provider"
- "Learn about peptide care"
- "See how it works"

Avoid CTA language that implies automatic eligibility or immediate treatment access, such as "Get peptides now," "Start treatment today," or "Buy peptide therapy."

## Content Improvements

The current page already has useful bones: a clear hero, three-step intro, consult card, program cards, process section, lifestyle section, form, and footer disclaimer. The next implementation pass should keep that structure but make the content safer, clearer, and more complete.

Recommended page structure:

1. Hero: concise provider-led review message, safer trust line, consult CTA, and education CTA.
2. Near-hero disclaimer: a short note that peptide therapies require review, may not be appropriate for everyone, and are not guaranteed.
3. Intro/process cards: keep a three-step flow, but broaden intake language to goals, routines, medical history, medications, symptoms or concerns, and questions.
4. Peptide section: reframe named peptide cards as consultation topics requiring clinical review, not benefit programs.
5. Education section: add a plain-language "What are peptides?" or "What to know before starting" block.
6. Lifestyle section: keep the "beyond the vial" concept, but avoid outcome promises.
7. FAQ section: add practical questions about eligibility, prescriptions, labs, administration, follow-up, and whether peptides are automatically prescribed.
8. Footer: retain the existing disclaimer and strengthen it if counsel or medical leadership approves final wording.

Recommended navigation improvements:

- Replace placeholder submenu items such as "Untitled option 1" with meaningful labels before launch.
- Consider navigation labels such as Blood Work, Peptides, Supplements, Process, FAQ, About, and Consult.
- If Clinics remains in navigation, clarify whether Prime Point has physical clinic locations or whether this is future-facing.

Recommended content additions:

- A short "How eligibility works" section.
- A "What your provider reviews" checklist: goals, health history, current medications, contraindications, lifestyle, prior labs if available, and questions.
- A "What a consult can and cannot do" section that clarifies prescriptions are only considered after review.
- A compact FAQ using conservative, educational language.
- A disclaimer near the peptide cards, not only in the footer.

## Legal-Safe Wording

Use wording that frames services as education, discussion, review, and individualized planning.

Safer phrases to use:

- "provider-led review"
- "licensed-provider evaluation"
- "when clinically appropriate"
- "if eligible"
- "may be discussed"
- "risks, limitations, and next steps"
- "based on health history, goals, medications, and clinical review"
- "results vary"
- "not appropriate for everyone"
- "educational information, not a diagnosis or guarantee of results"

Replace high-risk current wording:

- Replace "FDA-Regulated Labs • Pharma-Grade • Professional Oversight" with "Medical intake • Licensed-provider review • Prescription only when appropriate."
- Replace "Transform your health" with "Start with a provider-led review."
- Replace "peptides designed for weight loss, lean muscle, faster recovery, sexual health and peak performance" with "a provider-led discussion about your goals, medical history, risks, and appropriate options."
- Replace "Clinician-guided telehealth peptide programs" with "Provider-led peptide care reviews" or "Clinician-guided telehealth peptide consultations."
- Replace "For the everyday optimizer" with "For wellness-minded adults" or "For patients seeking clear guidance."
- Replace "Peptide access begins with a provider review" with "A provider review comes before any peptide therapy is considered."
- Replace "Better inputs, better outcomes" with "Better inputs, clearer guidance."
- Replace "Sleep optimization," "Metabolic wellness," and "Longevity support" with "Sleep and routine education," "Metabolic-health conversations," and "Healthy-aging education."

Named peptide guidance:

- Do not present BPC-157 or TP-500 as recovery, mobility, performance, muscle, sexual health, weight loss, or longevity programs.
- If named peptides remain on the page, frame them as topics that may be discussed during a clinical consultation.
- Pair named-peptide mentions with risk and limitation language.
- Use "provider-review required" as the tag rather than "recovery focus" or "performance lifestyle."

Safer named-peptide card direction:

"BPC-157 may be discussed during a clinical consultation when relevant to a patient's goals and medical history. A licensed provider will review potential risks, limitations, and whether any therapy is appropriate."

"TP-500 may be discussed as part of a provider-led consultation. Availability and appropriateness depend on clinical review, applicable law, and pharmacy standards."

Avoid or do not introduce without further review:

- Disease, cure, treatment, healing, or relief claims.
- "Guaranteed," "transformative," "rapid results," or "therapy that works."
- Specific timelines for results.
- Claims that peptides burn fat, build lean muscle, reverse aging, improve sexual function, regenerate tissue, repair cells, balance hormones, or speed recovery.
- Claims about FDA-regulated labs, pharma-grade sourcing, licensed pharmacies, CLIA labs, FDA-registered outsourcing facilities, or pharmacy standards unless the business can substantiate the exact statement.
- Statements implying compounded peptides are FDA-approved or equivalent to FDA-approved products.

## Design And Content Recommendations

Overall direction:

- Keep the visual tone premium, calm, and clinically credible.
- Prioritize clarity over hype.
- Make the first screen about provider review and eligibility, not outcomes.
- Use goal-based navigation as a user journey aid, but keep copy framed as "goals people discuss" rather than "conditions treated" or "benefits delivered."

Hero recommendations:

- Keep one strong hero CTA for consult conversion.
- Use a secondary CTA for education, such as "Learn about peptide care" or "See how it works."
- Add a short disclaimer or eligibility note close to the hero.
- Avoid regulatory proof badges unless every statement is verified.

Program section recommendations:

- Retitle "Peptide programs" to something like "Peptide care starts with clinical review."
- Keep the consult card as the first step.
- If keeping program cards, use them to explain consultation topics and review requirements.
- Avoid benefit bullets on named peptides. Replace with bullets like "Medical-history review," "Risk and limitation discussion," and "Prescription only if appropriate."

Education recommendations:

- Add a compact educational section explaining that peptides are short chains of amino acids and that different peptides have different uses, risks, evidence levels, and regulatory status.
- Distinguish general peptide education from provider-guided care.
- Include "supplements are not the same as prescription peptide medications" only if the site needs that distinction and the wording is reviewed.

FAQ recommendations:

- What are peptides?
- How does a provider decide whether peptide therapy is appropriate?
- Are peptides prescribed automatically after a consult?
- What health information may be reviewed?
- How are peptide therapies administered?
- What risks or limitations should be discussed?
- Who may not be a candidate?
- Is care available in every state?
- What happens after a provider review?

Trust signal recommendations:

- Licensed-provider oversight.
- Clear consultation-first process.
- Eligibility review before therapy is considered.
- Ongoing follow-up when prescribed.
- Secure telehealth language if accurate.
- Contact or support expectations.
- Transparent state availability if relevant.
- Named clinical leadership or provider credentials only if confirmed.
